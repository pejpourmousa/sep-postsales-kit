Purpose

One consistent maturity framework applied across all five post-sale teams — Customer Success, Account Management, Professional Services, Tech Support, and Customer Marketing — so leadership can compare maturity, track progress year over year (2024-2028), and target investment where it moves retention and expansion the most.

The 5 Categories

Every team tab is broken into the same five pillars, adapted from Sumeru's own CS Optimization Guidance framework: Customer (adoption, health, journey, voice of customer) · Financial (KPIs, forecasting, unit economics) · Practice (playbooks, tools, SOPs, governance) · Employee / Org (structure, roles, incentives, career path) · Enablement, Internal & External (training, self-service, AI tooling).

Sub-Categories

Most categories contain 4 sub-categories specific to that team's function; Practice has 5 and Employee (Org) has 6 on every tab (24 rows per tab total), each with its own 4-level maturity definition — not a generic scale reused across rows.

What's New — Forward-Looking Practices

Four sub-categories were added to every team tab to push the framework past pure process maturity toward where leading post-sale organizations are headed next: Unified Signal Integration (Customer) joins activity, account context, and performance data instead of leaving them siloed; AI Capability Gap Assessment & Augmentation Roadmap (Practice) makes AI adoption a deliberate, scored process rather than opportunistic tool-buying; and two Employee (Org) additions — Capacity Modeling & Utilization Governance and Performance Governance: Effort, Prioritization & Execution — bring data-driven bandwidth planning and a structured three-lens coaching model to replace gut-feel headcount and performance conversations. These four rows are written with the deepest, most specific guidance on the Customer Success and Account Management tabs, since that is where performance-management discipline compounds fastest into NRR.

Scoring Scale

Score every sub-category 1-4 for each year, or select N/A if it does not apply to that company/year. 1 = Ad Hoc / Reactive (no defined process, inconsistent, no ownership). 2 = Emerging / Developing (a process exists but is manual, inconsistent, or unmeasured). 3 = Defined / Managed (documented, consistent, measured, cross-functionally aligned). 4 = Optimized / Strategic (proactive, data/AI-driven, continuously improved, tied to business outcomes).

N/A Option

Use N/A for a sub-category that is genuinely not applicable to a given portfolio company or year (e.g., a company with no Professional Services offering). N/A cells are automatically excluded from all averages on the Summary Dashboard.

Target Column

A forward-looking target score (current or next year) for each sub-category, to compare actual vs. target progress.

Owner Column

The role typically accountable for that sub-category, pre-filled as a starting point — edit to match the specific portfolio company's org chart.

Notes / Evidence

Free text for supporting evidence, links, or context behind a score — use this to justify why a score moved.

Customer Journey Design & Ownership

A mapped, owned journey turns onboarding into renewal into a repeatable motion instead of one-off saves.

L1

No documented customer journey exists; CSMs improvise onboarding, adoption, and renewal touchpoints account by account. Handoffs between sales, onboarding, and support happen ad hoc with no defined trigger points or owner. Customers experience inconsistent engagement depending on which CSM they draw.

L2

A journey map exists in a slide deck but is not embedded in day-to-day workflow or the CRM. Some CSMs follow it loosely; stages and milestones are not consistently tracked or triggered by data. Handoff points between teams are named but not enforced.

L3

The journey is documented with defined stages, milestones, and triggers, built into the CRM/CS platform, and used consistently across the team. Cross-functional handoffs (sales to onboarding to CSM) are formalized with clear ownership and SLAs at each stage. Journey adherence is tracked and reviewed in team operating rhythms.

L4

The journey is instrumented end-to-end inside a One Customer View that blends CRM, product telemetry, support, and call data, so stage transitions trigger automatically rather than being tracked manually. Journey performance is benchmarked against retention and expansion outcomes and refined continuously based on what predicts churn or growth. CSMs spend their time on judgment calls, not on manually tracking where an account sits.

Customer Health & Pulse Scoring

A reliable health score is the single leading indicator CSMs and leadership use to spot churn before it happens.

L1

Account health is a CSM's gut feel, not a score; there is no defined model, no dimensions, and no consistent update cadence. Different CSMs use different informal signals, so the same account can be called healthy by one CSM and at-risk by another.

L2

A health score exists with one or two dimensions (usually usage), calculated manually or in a spreadsheet on an inconsistent cadence. It is owned loosely by CSMs but not fed by product, support, or finance data, and leadership does not trust it enough to act on it.

L3

A documented health score with three to six dimensions (usage, sponsor engagement, multi-team engagement, support volume, C.SAT) is owned by the CSM but fed automatically by product and support systems. Scores are refreshed on a defined cadence, banded from extremely satisfied to severe risk, and reviewed in regular account and leadership meetings.

L4

Health scoring is AI-driven, surfacing risk and opportunity signals automatically from the One Customer View rather than waiting for a periodic manual refresh, with every signal requiring a logged CSM action. Score accuracy is continuously validated against actual churn and expansion outcomes, and the model is retrained as new signals prove predictive. Leadership uses the score as a real-time input to portfolio-level retention forecasting.

At-Risk Account Review Process

Catching risk early and reviewing it consistently is what turns a save into a routine outcome instead of luck.

L1

There is no formal at-risk process; accounts get flagged only when a customer escalates or threatens to churn. Reviews, when they happen, are one-off fire drills with no consistent participants or documented plan.

L2

An at-risk list exists but is compiled manually and reviewed inconsistently, often only for the largest accounts. Escalation criteria are informal, save plans vary by CSM, and there is no consistent tracking of whether interventions worked.

L3

Defined at-risk criteria and an escalation process trigger a structured internal account review with a documented save plan, clear owner, and executive visibility for material accounts. Reviews happen on a fixed cadence, outcomes are tracked, and the process covers the full at-risk population, not just the largest logos.

L4

At-risk accounts are surfaced automatically by AI-driven signal detection the moment a risk pattern emerges, well ahead of the traditional review cadence, and save-plan effectiveness is measured and fed back into the risk model. Segment-specific timing rules govern when an automated alert triggers a human review versus a lighter-touch automated nudge. Save-rate and root-cause data are reviewed by leadership to fix systemic drivers of churn, not just individual accounts.

Voice of Customer Program (C.SAT/NPS)

Structured feedback loops validate whether the account actually feels the value the health score assumes.

L1

No formal C.SAT or NPS program exists; feedback comes in informally through emails or calls that are never aggregated. There is no target, no survey cadence, and no way to compare performance across accounts or over time.

L2

C.SAT or NPS surveys are sent inconsistently, often only after a support case or renewal, with response rates too low to trust. Results are collected but not systematically shared back with CSMs, product, or leadership.

L3

C.SAT (0-5 scale) and NPS (buyer and end-user) are measured on a consistent cadence with defined targets, currently 4.6+ C.SAT and 30+ buyer NPS, 5+ end-user NPS. Results are segmented, reviewed cross-functionally, and closed-loop follow-up is required on detractor responses.

L4

Feedback data feeds directly into the health score and account risk model rather than sitting in a separate report, and detractor patterns are analyzed for systemic product or process fixes. Targets step up year over year (4.7+ C.SAT) and results are benchmarked against industry norms to guide where CS invests next. Leadership treats VoC trends as a leading indicator of NRR, not a lagging satisfaction metric.

Unified Signal Integration (Activity, Context & Performance)

New

CSMs act off partial pictures when activity data, customer context, and outcome performance sit in separate systems; unifying them is what actually predicts risk and opportunity.

L1

CSM activity (calls, emails, tickets touched), customer context (usage, goals, org changes), and performance outcomes (NRR, renewal, expansion) live in disconnected tools with no shared view. CSMs manually reconcile these before every account conversation, and nothing links what a CSM did to what actually happened to the account.

L2

Some of the three domains are connected pairwise, for example activity logged in the CRM alongside basic usage data, but performance outcomes are reported separately and on a lag. There is no single place a CSM or manager can see effort, context, and result together for a given account.

L3

Activity, context, and performance data are joined in a shared reporting layer, refreshed on a regular cadence, so managers can see which CSM actions correlate with which account outcomes. This joined view is a standing input to account reviews and coaching, not a one-off analysis.

L4

Activity, context, and performance signals are fused in real time inside the One Customer View, and a "next best action" layer continuously ranks accounts and recommends the highest-impact CSM action rather than leaving prioritization to instinct. Leadership can trace, for any point in time, exactly how CSM hours converted into NRR outcomes, and the model is retrained as new signal-to-outcome patterns emerge.

Core Retention & C.SAT KPI Reporting

Without a consistent Tier 1 KPI set, leadership cannot tell if CS performance is improving or just noisy.

L1

KPIs are pulled ad hoc for board meetings with no standard definitions; gross retention, net retention, and renewal rate are calculated differently depending on who builds the deck. There is no regular cadence or owner for CS financial reporting.

L2

A basic KPI set (retention, renewal rate, C.SAT) is tracked but calculated manually in spreadsheets, with definitions that shift over time and reconciliation issues against finance's numbers. Reporting happens around board cycles rather than on an operating cadence.

L3

Tier 1 KPIs (gross retention LTM, net retention, gross retention hard-churn-only, L3M renewal rate, ARR up for renewal, C.SAT) and Tier 2 KPIs (support cost % of ARR, PS margin, case metrics) are defined once, calculated consistently, and reported on a monthly operating cadence owned by CS Ops. Finance and CS reconcile numbers so the board sees one version of the truth.

L4

KPIs are live in a dashboard fed by the One Customer View, with drivers decomposed automatically (which segment, which cohort, which reason code is moving retention) rather than requiring a manual deep-dive. Targets are benchmarked against portfolio peers and prior-year performance, and KPI movement automatically triggers the relevant playbook or review, such as a renewal-rate dip triggering an at-risk sweep. NRR functions as the team's north-star metric tying CS directly to enterprise value.

Renewal Forecasting & Outreach Process

A disciplined forecast and outreach cadence is what protects revenue instead of discovering churn after the fact.

L1

Renewals are tracked informally per CSM with no consolidated forecast; outreach timing is whatever each CSM remembers to do. There is no visibility for leadership into which renewals are at risk until they are already late.

L2

A renewal forecast spreadsheet exists and is updated inconsistently; forecast calls happen sporadically and outreach timelines vary by CSM and account manager. Forecast accuracy is not tracked against actual outcomes.

L3

A defined forecast call model runs on a fixed cadence with CSMs, AMs, and leadership, using consistent risk categories and standard outreach timelines by segment (e.g., 120/90/60/30-day touchpoints). Forecast accuracy is measured against actuals and used to improve the model.

L4

Renewal timing and outreach triggers are automated by segment-specific rules rather than manually scheduled, with a manager review layer that checks AI-driven risk calls before they reach the customer. Forecast accuracy is tracked at the cohort level and continuously tightened, and the model flags emerging risk patterns before they show up in a standard forecast call. Renewal performance feeds directly back into pricing, packaging, and capacity planning decisions.

CSM Capacity & Coverage Model

Right-sized capacity determines whether CSMs can be proactive or are permanently stuck firefighting the biggest accounts.

L1

Account assignment and headcount planning are done by feel; there is no capacity model, so some CSMs are overloaded while others carry light books with no clear rationale. Hiring decisions are reactive to attrition or complaints, not planned.

L2

A basic book-size guideline exists (e.g., accounts or ARR per CSM) but is applied inconsistently and does not account for segment complexity or account tier. Capacity planning happens once a year rather than being revisited as the book changes.

L3

A documented capacity model sets ratios by segment and account complexity, is reviewed on a regular cadence against headcount and ARR growth, and explicitly balances CSM FTEs against contractor use. Hiring and reallocation decisions are tied directly to the model.

L4

Capacity modeling is dynamic, adjusting CSM allocation based on real-time account health and workload signals from the One Customer View rather than a static annual ratio. The model is benchmarked against comparable SaaS businesses and used to make the ROI case for headcount investment tied to retention and margin outcomes. Coverage decisions actively optimize for where CSM time drives the most NRR impact.

Product Adoption Measurement & PS-to-Subscription Conversion

Adoption is the leading driver of retention, and measuring it is what makes upsell and renewal conversations evidence-based.

L1

There is no consistent way to measure product adoption; CSMs rely on anecdotal impressions from calls rather than usage data. Conversion of PS engagements into ongoing subscription services is untracked and happens inconsistently deal by deal.

Adoption data flows automatically into the health score and account plan through the One Customer View, and low-adoption accounts trigger proactive enablement plays before they become renewal risk. PS-to-SS conversion is forecast and optimized using historical conversion patterns by segment, and adoption trends directly inform product roadmap prioritization. Adoption is tracked as a leading indicator of NRR, not a lagging usage report.

Repeatable Playbooks & Central Repository

Without a shared playbook library, every CSM reinvents the same onboarding, QBR, and save motions from scratch.

L1

No central repository or playbooks exist; each CSM keeps their own templates and approach, often not documented anywhere shareable. Best practices discovered by one CSM never make it to the rest of the team.

L2

Some playbooks and templates exist but live scattered across personal drives and folders, are not version-controlled, and go stale without anyone responsible for updating them. Adoption of the playbooks is inconsistent across the team.

L3

A central repository holds documented, current playbooks for the core CS motions (onboarding, QBR, at-risk save, renewal, expansion), owned by CS Ops, reviewed on a defined cadence, and consistently used across the team. New playbooks are added as new repeatable scenarios emerge.

L4

Playbooks are embedded directly into CSM workflow tools and triggered automatically by account signals, such as a health-score drop auto-surfacing the relevant save playbook, rather than requiring a CSM to remember to go look one up. Playbook effectiveness is measured against outcomes and the library is continuously refined, retiring what doesn't work and scaling what does. The repository functions as the operating system of the CS practice, not a reference library.

Kickoff-to-EBR Delivery Cadence

A consistent kickoff and EBR cadence is what makes value realization visible instead of assumed.

L1

Kickoffs and executive business reviews happen inconsistently, if at all, and with no standard agenda or documented outcomes. Whether an account gets an EBR depends entirely on whether the CSM thinks to schedule one.

L2

A kickoff and EBR template exists but scheduling and follow-through are inconsistent, tracked informally if at all, and quality varies widely by CSM. There is no visibility for leadership into which accounts are current on their cadence.

L3

Kickoff and EBR cadence is documented by segment (frequency, required attendees, standard agenda), tracked in the CS platform, and monitored by CS Ops for compliance across the book. Outcomes and action items from EBRs are logged and followed up on consistently.

EBR cadence and content adapt dynamically to account health and lifecycle stage, with prep auto-generated from the One Customer View (usage trends, open risks, roadmap fit) instead of a CSM manually assembling a deck. High-value accounts get executive-sponsor-level EBRs as a matter of course, not exception.

Automation Governance for CS Signals & Alerts

Governance turns automated signals into trusted, actionable alerts instead of ignored noise or unchecked AI decisions.

L1

Any automated alerts or scoring rules that exist were built ad hoc by individuals, with no defined owner, no documented logic, and no review of whether they're accurate. CSMs either ignore automated signals entirely or, in a few cases, act on them without any check on whether the underlying logic is sound.

L2

Some automated signals exist, such as a usage-drop alert, but ownership is unclear, timing rules are one-size-fits-all regardless of segment, and there's no process to confirm the automation is actually driving action rather than being ignored. AI-influenced recommendations, where they exist, are not reviewed by a manager before reaching a customer-facing decision.

L3

Every automated process has a named owner, documented logic, and explicit timing rules by segment (for example, different alert thresholds for enterprise vs. SMB), and there is a defined process requiring a logged action on every triggered signal. Managers periodically review a sample of AI-driven outcomes, not just usage volume, to confirm the automation is actually correct and helping.

L4

Automation governance operates as a continuous system: every AI-surfaced signal requires a logged action, mirroring the industry benchmark of roughly 80% action-rate on AI-surfaced signals, manager review of outcomes happens systematically rather than by spot check, and the signal models are retrained based on which alerts actually predicted real risk or opportunity. Governance scales automation volume, hundreds of signals in weeks rather than months, without losing trust in the output, because ownership, timing rules, and review are built into the system design itself. This governance layer is what lets the team safely push further into AI-driven, proactive CS.

AI Capability Gap Assessment & Augmentation Roadmap

New

Without a structured view of where AI could help, CS either under-invests or bets on the wrong use case first.

L1

There is no inventory of where AI could realistically augment CS work; AI tools, if any, were adopted opportunistically based on a vendor pitch rather than an assessed gap. No one owns evaluating AI opportunity against the team's actual bottlenecks.

L2

A rough list of "places AI might help" exists informally, usually generated in a single workshop, but it is not prioritized against effort, impact, or data readiness, and it is not revisited as tools and internal data maturity change.

L3

A documented AI-gap assessment scores candidate use cases (health scoring, save-plan drafting, EBR prep, coaching insight) against impact and feasibility, is owned by CS Ops, and feeds a prioritized 12-month augmentation roadmap reviewed with leadership.

L4

The AI-gap assessment is a living, data-informed process: each rolled-out use case is measured against the original impact estimate, gaps are re-scored as internal data quality and vendor capability improve, and the roadmap is benchmarked against what comparable CS orgs are automating. CS treats AI-readiness itself, meaning data quality, governance, and adoption, as a tracked capability, not just a list of tools.

Org Structure & Coverage Model

The org structure determines whether CS can execute the strategy or is structurally set up to fail it.

L1

The CS org has grown organically with no defined structure; reporting lines, pod boundaries, and segment coverage are unclear and shift with whoever is currently in the room. Roles overlap or leave gaps with no one accountable for noticing.

L3

The org structure is documented, explicitly aligned to account segmentation (tier, ARR, complexity), and reviewed on a regular cadence against business needs. Reporting lines, pod structures, and escalation paths are clear and consistently applied across the team.

L4

The structure is treated as a lever to be optimized, not a fixed chart, with coverage models continuously tested against retention and NRR outcomes by segment and adjusted as the business mix shifts, for example toward strategic accounts. The team benchmarks its structure against comparable SaaS peers and evolves toward a business-partner model where CS is structured around strategic account advisory, not just support delivery. Structure changes are planned proactively ahead of growth, not reactively after strain.

Roles & Responsibilities Definition

Clear role boundaries prevent dropped handoffs between CSM, AM, Support, and Sales that quietly erode the customer experience.

L1

Role boundaries are undefined; CSMs, AMs, and support all do a bit of everything with no documented RACI. Customers get inconsistent answers depending on who they reach, and internal disputes over whose account it is are common.

The RACI is a living document tied to strategy, not a static onboarding artifact.

Incentive & Compensation Plans

Incentives are the fastest way to tell a CSM what actually matters, whatever the strategy deck says.

L1

CSMs are paid flat salary or an ad hoc bonus with no link to retention, adoption, or C.SAT outcomes. Comp plans vary person to person based on individual negotiation rather than a defined framework.

L2

L4

Incentive design is actively tested and refined based on which metrics actually predict retention and NRR, with plans differentiated by segment, growth-weighted for expansion accounts and retention-weighted for renewal-heavy accounts. Comp outcomes are benchmarked against industry data and adjusted to attract and retain top CS talent as compensation becomes a competitive differentiator. Plans evolve as the org matures toward NRR-accountable, business-partner roles.

Goals, Targets & Performance Review Process

Without individual goals tied to team KPIs, performance management is subjective and retention accountability has no owner.

Goal frameworks are benchmarked and refined year over year as the team matures toward NRR accountability.

Capacity Modeling & Utilization Governance

New

Headcount requests without evidence of where hours actually go get rejected or overcorrected; data-driven capacity modeling protects both CSMs and the budget.

L1

CSM workload is invisible; managers estimate who is overloaded by complaints or attrition, and headcount requests are justified by anecdote rather than data. There is no way to see how CSM hours are actually split across accounts or activity types.

L3

CSM time is tracked by activity type and account, reconciled against the book-size model on a regular cadence, and reviewed by managers to catch both overload and underutilization before they become attrition or coverage risk. Capacity data, not gut feel, backs every headcount conversation with finance.

L4

Utilization is modeled continuously against NRR contribution, showing exactly where CSM hours are going relative to the revenue and retention they protect, and surfacing untapped bandwidth before a headcount request is even drafted. Burnout risk is flagged proactively from workload and sentiment signals, and capacity models are benchmarked against portfolio peers to make an evidence-based case for investment.

Performance Governance: Effort, Prioritization & Execution

New

Coaching on gut feel from a handful of observed calls misses most of what a CSM actually does; a structured effort/prioritization/execution lens makes coaching evidence-based.

L1

Coaching is based on whatever a manager happened to observe directly, typically a small fraction of a CSM's actual activity, with no structured way to separate how much a CSM is doing, what they're choosing to focus on, and how well they're executing it. 1:1s are generic check-ins, not diagnostic conversations.

L2

Managers informally sense whether a CSM is "busy" or "behind," but there is no defined framework separating effort, prioritization, and execution, so feedback tends to collapse into a single vague impression rather than a specific, actionable coaching point.

L3

1:1s and performance reviews explicitly separate effort (activity volume against expected coverage), prioritization (whether time is going to the highest-risk or highest-opportunity accounts), and execution (quality and outcome of the actions taken). Managers walk into every 1:1 with data on all three dimensions, not just impression.

L4

Effort, prioritization, and execution are scored from the unified activity and outcome data continuously, not reconstructed manually before each 1:1, letting managers coach the specific dimension that is actually holding a CSM back. Team-level patterns across the three dimensions inform training investment, and this governance approach is what closes the gap that separates best-in-class from gut-feel performance management (McKinsey research cites roughly 15 points of NRR difference).

Sales/CS Methodology Alignment

Misaligned methodology between Sales and CS creates the handoff gaps where new customers churn before they ever adopt.

L1

CS has no formal methodology of its own and no alignment with the sales methodology; onboarding starts from whatever the sales rep happened to promise, documented or not. There's no shared language between Sales and CS for qualifying account risk or value.

L2

A CS methodology exists on paper, such as a standard onboarding checklist, but is not actively aligned with how Sales qualifies and sells, so expectations set pre-sale often don't match delivery. Training on the methodology happens once at hire and is not reinforced.

L3

A defined CS methodology is explicitly mapped to the sales methodology, with shared terminology, staged handoff criteria, and joint training so Sales and CS operate off the same playbook. Adherence is reinforced through coaching and periodic refreshers.

L4

Methodology alignment is continuously tuned using deal and account outcome data, feeding learnings from renewal and churn patterns back into how Sales qualifies and positions deals in the first place.
